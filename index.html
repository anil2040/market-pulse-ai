📊 Macro Insights · what this morning's news adds up to

  • The economy is growing faster than the Fed assumed at year-start, pushing the neutral interest rate higher and justifying 10-year Treasury yields at 5.33%, their highest since 2002. This repricing reflects real economic strength, not inflation panic, as the Fed now believes its current 3.88% rate sits below neutral and may need to rise further over the next 12 months.
  • Stock market wealth—now $74 trillion in household holdings, a record—is doing nearly all the economic heavy lifting. Half of current consumption growth flows from the wealth effect of rising equity values, while real wage growth remains negative for lower-income households and inflation stays stubbornly above target at 3.7% headline and 3.0% core.
  • Manufacturing remains robust with the ISM at 54.5 and 14 consecutive months of expansion, yet jobless claims at 197,000 are historically low and employment is resilient. Consumer sentiment jumped to 51.7 from 44.8 three months ago, but workers report jobs are harder to find than at any point in five years—a jarring disconnect between headline strength and lived experience.
  • Treasury yields now create structural headwinds for rate-sensitive sectors: real estate yields negative real returns at 5.3%, utilities lose dividend appeal, and the bond market posted its worst quarter since 2022. Small-cap stocks with floating-rate debt and high-growth names dependent on low rates face persistent pressure.
  • Oil prices have risen 48% from 12-month lows ($62.6 to $92.8) on Iran tensions and Chinese export suspensions, feeding manufacturing input-cost inflation and reducing confidence that core PCE will fall to the Fed's 2% target soon. Energy sector outperformance masks broad fragility in rate-sensitive growth.
  • The Fed has cover to pause or move slowly on rate hikes through the Q4 election cycle, especially after Fed Vice Chair John Williams signaled "no need for urgency." October rate-hike odds have fallen to 38% from 76%, but December could shift if economic weakness becomes apparent; the central bank is buying time rather than committing to a pause.
  • Credit stress is building quietly: high-yield spreads widened to 312 basis points from 274 three months ago, and the yield curve remains flat at just 46 basis points, a recession warning if it inverts. The Fed's higher-for-longer posture is now visible in credit markets even as equities remain buoyant.
  • Valuation extremes persist with CAPE at 41.1 (2.4x historical average) while the world ex-US trades at 18x earnings. The US premium reflects AI euphoria and concentrated mega-cap concentration, not fundamentals; any shock to the wealth effect or equity multiple compression would expose how much of the economic story depends on paper gains.
  • Tech earnings (Micron, Alphabet) are driving sentiment, but these gains are largely priced in and rest on sustained data-center capex that hinges on unproven AI monetization and continued margin expansion in a high-rate environment. A slowdown in AI spending or earnings disappointment could trigger rapid multiple mean reversion.
  • The week ahead brings September jobs data (Friday), which will be the clearest signal of labor-market deceleration or resilience. A significant miss vs. consensus of +100k payrolls could accelerate Fed pause bets; a beat would reinforce the case for higher rates and greater economic strength relative to inflation, widening the credibility gap between headline data and the mood of ordinary workers.
